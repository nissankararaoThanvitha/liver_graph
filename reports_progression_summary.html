Liver knowledge graph · findings

Genes that track liver disease getting worse

We searched 14,794 genes across 1,027 patients from eight separate studies, and found the ones that rise and fall as the liver deteriorates — plus the drugs that already target them.

Eight public liver studies were cleaned and combined. Each study named its genes differently, so all of them were translated into one naming system — 99.2% matched. Each study also recorded patient information differently, so those were standardised too.

We then added PrimeKG, the Harvard knowledge graph built from 20 biological databases. It brought in 17,080 diseases, 6,642 drugs and 2,516 pathways, connected to our genes by 837,337 links. Three of the four databases requested are inside it: Reactome, DrugBank and CTD.

What we asked

One question, asked of every gene

Does this gene rise steadily as the liver gets worse?

Liver disease moves along two separate ladders, and doctors score them separately because they are not the same thing. 40 of our patients have full-blown inflammation but no scarring yet — the most treatable moment in the disease, and invisible if you only look at scarring. So we ran the analysis twice, once per ladder.

To avoid false results, each gene was tested separately inside every study, and only kept when every study agreed. A gene that behaves well in one study but not the others is a quirk of that study, not biology.

Why we believe the result

Two independent checks

1. The computer found the right biology on its own

The analysis is pure mathematics on numbers — it has no idea what any gene does. Yet the genes it ranked highest for scarring (THY1, LAMC3, THBS2, COL1A1, LUM) are the exact genes already known to cause liver scarring.

And the genes going down — ACADSB, SLC27A5, HAAO — are all liver metabolism enzymes. As the liver scars, it loses its normal job. That is what the biology says should happen.

2. One of our top genes has a published paper about it

Our scarring list put THBS2 near the top. One of the eight studies we used has a published paper titled:

“Transcriptomics Identify Thrombospondin‑2 as a Biomarker for NASH and Advanced Liver Fibrosis”

Published in Hepatology, 2021. Our method rediscovered a peer-reviewed finding without being told to look for it.

Galectin‑3 (LGALS3) is the standout — it is already in clinical trials as an anti-scarring drug for liver disease, and our patients confirm it rises on both ladders.

The graph also surfaced Nintedanib, a drug approved for lung scarring, which targets six genes raised in our cirrhosis patients (PDGFRA, PDGFRB, SRC, FGFR2, FGFR3, LCK). It is not approved for liver disease. Scarring works similarly across organs, so this is a genuine repurposing lead.
